# Slide Writing for Investor Pitch Decks

Slides must be readable in five seconds. Every word must earn its place. Investors scan decks; they don't read them like documents.

## The Three Design Laws (Y Combinator)

1. **Legible** — readable by "an older person with bad eyesight from the back row": large type, simple typeface, high contrast, dark text on white.
2. **Simple** — one slide, one idea. Pick the 5–7 key ideas the investor must remember and build the deck around only those. After a short pitch, people genuinely retain 1–2 ideas.
3. **Obvious** — a stranger must understand each slide at a glance. If a slide needs a second read, rewrite it. No subtle humor, insider metaphors, or "figure it out yourself" charts.

Remember: investors invest in the team, not the slides. Slides only carry the message.

## Bullet Point Rules

### Quantity and Length

- **3–5 bullets per slide**, each under 12 words
- Prefer fragments over sentences
- If you need more than 5 bullets, split the slide
- If a bullet exceeds 12 words, split it into two bullets or two slides

### Content Quality

- **Lead with the concrete**: numbers, names, examples
- Remove adjectives that do not carry information ("innovative", "seamless", "cutting-edge", "revolutionary", "game-changing")
- Every bullet must be a fact or a claim you can defend
- No vague statements like "we leverage AI" — say what the AI does and why it matters

### Examples

**Bad:**

- "Our innovative platform seamlessly integrates cutting-edge AI"
- "Game-changing solution for enterprise customers"

**Good:**

- "Contractors spend 6 hours/week chasing payments across 3 platforms"
- "AI agents complete trial tasks in 30 minutes vs 6 hours for humans"
- "Enterprise customers save $400/week per contractor"

## Big Number Slides

A single strong number deserves its own slide with a one-line label. Use big-number slides for:

- Key metric (users, revenue, growth rate)
- Impact number (time saved, cost reduction, efficiency gain)
- Milestone (customers served, contracts signed)

**Structure:**

- Large number (28pt+) as the visual focal point
- One-line label below it
- 2–3 supporting bullets maximum
- Example: "$400/week" as the big number, "Lost per contractor to scheduling chaos" as the label

## Speaker Notes

Every slide must have speaker notes. Notes carry the explanation the slide leaves out.

### Purpose of Notes

- Explain the slide in 1–3 sentences
- Provide talking points for the presenter
- Add context that doesn't fit on the slide
- Reference specific data points or examples

### What to Include

- **Title slide**: introduce yourself and the work in 2–3 sentences
- **Background**: state why the problem matters and what gap you address
- **Methods**: explain the design in plain language, highlight what's novel
- **Results**: walk through the numbers, point to the table/figure, state what it means
- **Discussion**: contextualise against prior work, note limitations honestly
- **Closing**: summarise the one thing the audience should remember

### What to Avoid

- Generic placeholder notes ("This slide shows our market size")
- Reading the slide bullets aloud (notes should add, not repeat)
- Overly detailed scripts (1–3 sentences per slide)

## Vocabulary and Terminology

- **Use the audience's vocabulary**; define any term a newcomer would not know
- Avoid jargon unless you define it immediately
- If you must use technical terms, explain them in the notes
- Never assume the investor knows your industry's acronyms

**Examples:**

- Instead of "We optimize LTV/CAC ratios", say "We earn $3 for every $1 we spend on customer acquisition"
- Instead of "Our NDR is 140%", say "Customers who spend $100 in month 1 spend $140 in month 2"

## The Closing Slide

The closing slide repeats the one sentence you want remembered.

- One declarative sentence (the core message)
- Contact information (email, website)
- Call to action (what happens next)
- No new information

**Example:**

- "Moot lets companies audition AI agents on real work before hiring them, reducing bad hires by 60%."
- Contact: founders@moot.com
- Call to action: "Let's run a pilot with your team"

## Form Rules

### Word Count

- **≤ ~50 words per slide**; any slide needing >30 seconds to parse is too complex
- If a slide exceeds 50 words, split it or cut content

### Text Density

- Leave white space — crowded slides are hard to read
- Don't fill every inch with text
- Use the slide title + bullets structure, not paragraphs

### Readability

- Dark text on light background (WCAG AA contrast: 4.5:1 minimum)
- Minimum 15pt body text for live presentations
- Minimum 18pt for emailed decks
- No light text on dark backgrounds (hard to read on projectors)

## Anti-Patterns

### Content

- Paragraphs instead of bullets
- Sentences instead of fragments
- Buzzwords without substance
- Numbers without context (what does this number mean?)
- Claims without evidence

### Structure

- More than 5 bullets per slide
- Bullets over 12 words each
- Nested bullets (bullets within bullets)
- Multiple levels of hierarchy

### Style

- All-caps text (hard to read, looks like shouting)
- Underlining (use bold or color for emphasis instead)
- Italics for body text (use for quotes or emphasis only)
- More than 2 fonts or 2 colors per slide

## Revision Checklist

When reviewing a slide, ask:

1. **Can I read this in 5 seconds?**
2. **Does every bullet advance the main idea?**
3. **Are there more than 5 bullets?**
4. **Is any bullet over 12 words?**
5. **Are there meaningless adjectives?**
6. **Does the slide have speaker notes?**
7. **Is the contrast sufficient?**
8. **Would a stranger understand this without explanation?**

If the answer to any of these is "no", rewrite the slide.
